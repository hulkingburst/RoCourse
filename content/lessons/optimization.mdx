Your game works. Now make it smooth. Performance is a budget, and this lesson shows you where beginner games spend it wastefully — and how to measure before you fix anything.

<Step>

## Smooth is a budget

A game running at 60 FPS has about **16 milliseconds** per frame to do everything — physics, scripts, rendering. Cross the budget and frames drop:

- If the **server** scripts lag, the game feels slow for every player.
- If the **client** lags, the player's game drops frames.

Optimization is spending that budget on things the player actually notices.

## Measure before you change

The #1 optimization rule: **don't guess what's slow.** Guessing wastes hours and often fixes nothing.

Open **View → Stats** in Studio and watch three numbers while you play:

- **Physics** — part count, and how often parts move or collide.
- **Script** — how much time your Lua code spends per frame.
- **Render** — how many things are being drawn.

<Note>
Roblox ships a deeper tool, the **MicroProfiler**, that breaks down every task per frame. You don't need it yet — the Stats window is enough to find beginner problems.
</Note>

The same three costs eat beginner games over and over: **parts, loops, and events.** Fix those and you've fixed most of it.

## Parts: every part is a bill

Each part costs memory, physics, and rendering time — even if it never moves. A field of 500 tiny coins is 500 bills.

Ways the cost grows fast:

- Hundreds of small **unanchored** parts (each one is simulated by the physics engine).
- Parts created every second and never destroyed (explosions, projectiles, effects).
- Scripts calling `Instance.new("Part")` inside loops.

The fix is rarely "no parts." It's **fewer, smarter parts**:

- Anchor anything that never moves.
- **Reuse** a pool of projectiles and effects instead of creating fresh ones.
- `part:Destroy()` parts when they're done — don't just hide them.

<FillBlank
  prompt="A part that never moves should be ____ so the physics engine stops simulating it."
  answer="anchored"
  explanation="Anchored parts are still rendered, but the physics engine stops tracking them — a huge save on part-heavy games."
/>

</Step>

## Loops: throttle or spin

A bare `while true do` body runs as fast as Luau can — often hundreds of times per second. If that body does anything at all, you're burning the frame budget on work nobody sees.

```luau
-- ❌ spins flat-out, burning CPU
while true do
    print("still here")
end

-- ✅ does the same job, twice a second
while true do
    print("still here")
    task.wait(0.5)
end
```

Rule of thumb: put a `task.wait` in **every** loop, and make the delay as large as the job allows. Decorative loops and UI tweens can wait 0.1–0.5 s; player-facing logic can be snappier.

## Events: disconnect what you don't need

Connections are cheap to make and cheap to leave — which is exactly how they pile up:

- A listener created every time a player's character spawns, and never disconnected.
- A RemoteEvent fired every frame so the client can watch a number update.

The pattern to learn: **connect once, disconnect when done.**

```luau
local connection = part.Touched:Connect(onTouch)
-- when the part is gone, so is the connection:
connection:Disconnect()
```

## Common mistakes

- **Optimizing before measuring.** Change nothing until Studio Stats shows the problem.
- **Waiting 0.001 out of fear.** Tiny delays still spin; pick a delay that matches the job.
- **Destroying without disconnecting.** If a part dies but its listeners stay, they fire at nothing forever.
- **Rewriting everything.** First pass: fix the three costs — parts, loops, events. Only then touch deeper scripts.
